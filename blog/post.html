How AI is rewriting the way software gets built from scratch

We're at an inflection point. The tools developers use to ship software are changing faster than most teams can adapt — and the companies that figure it out first will define the next decade.

Eric Simons
CEO, StackBlitz
Share Copy link

Four years ago, if you wanted to build a web application, you needed to understand webpack, configure a local dev environment, manage dependencies, and know enough about deployment pipelines to not break production. Today, you can describe what you want in plain English and have something running in under five minutes.

That's not an exaggeration — it's something we see happening every day across the 4 million apps built on Bolt. The barrier to entry has dropped so fast that entire categories of knowledge that used to be prerequisites are now optional. And that changes everything about how software teams are structured, how products get shipped, and what it means to be a developer.

The compression of the stack

The most significant shift isn't that AI writes code. It's that AI compresses the distance between intent and output. A product manager can now sketch a feature and have something testable within an hour. A designer can prototype interactions without writing a line of JavaScript. An engineer can scaffold an entire backend in the time it used to take to set up a database connection.

"The bottleneck was never the idea. It was always the distance between what you imagined and what you could ship."

— Eric Simons, CEO StackBlitz

This compression has a compounding effect. When iteration cycles collapse from days to minutes, teams learn faster. They can test more hypotheses, kill bad ideas sooner, and double down on what's working. The companies building with AI-native tools aren't just shipping faster — they're compounding their learning at a rate that's difficult to match with traditional workflows.

What this means for engineering teams

The role of the software engineer isn't disappearing — it's evolving. The engineers who will thrive in this environment are the ones who understand how to direct AI effectively, who can evaluate generated code critically, and who know when to override the machine.

That requires a different kind of expertise. Less time spent on boilerplate, more time spent on architecture decisions. Less debugging syntax errors, more thinking about system design. The craft doesn't disappear — it moves up the stack.

The skills that matter now

  • Prompt engineering — knowing how to communicate intent clearly and iterate on outputs
  • Systems thinking — understanding how components interact at a higher level
  • Critical evaluation — recognising when generated code is subtly wrong
  • Product intuition — the thing AI still can't replicate: knowing what to build

The opportunity ahead

We're still in the earliest innings of this transition. The tools are improving at a pace that makes 12-month-old predictions feel dated. What feels fast today will feel slow by the end of the year.

The question for every team isn't whether to adopt AI-native development — it's how to do it in a way that actually changes your output, rather than just your workflow. That's a harder problem than it sounds. It requires rethinking how you structure teams, how you evaluate progress, and what you consider a meaningful unit of work.

The teams that figure this out won't just ship faster. They'll build things that weren't previously possible — because the constraints that used to define what was feasible no longer apply in the same way.

That's the opportunity in front of us. And it's larger than most people realise.
